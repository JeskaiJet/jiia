<Section title="Why Dining Subscription Is More Than a Membership Benefit">
<WideImage src="/images/tabmac/tabmac_flow.webp" alt="User flow" width={1920} height={1080} />

Dining is not naturally a subscription-based behavior. Most dining decisions happen as separate moments: what to eat today, which restaurant to choose, how much to spend, and whether a new place is worth trying. For users, that means repeated discovery and comparison. For restaurants, demand is hard to turn into predictable repeat relationships.

Tabmac's opportunity was not to add another membership layer for restaurants, but to reorganize fragmented dining consumption into a subscription model. Through Standard, Pro, Max, Ultra, and dining credits, users are not buying a one-time discount at one restaurant. They are buying dining capacity that can be used continuously and combined freely across a partner restaurant network.

So Tabmac is not fundamentally about "selling plans." It is about building a subscription-based dining network: users understand and use dining value through credits, restaurants gain a more stable demand entry point, and later business purchasing, venue booking, and in-store fulfillment can all build around the same credit system.
</Section>

<Section title="Putting Consumers, Businesses, and Restaurants into One Value Chain">
Tabmac's business logic is not about serving consumers, businesses, and restaurants separately. It is about putting them into the same value chain through one dining credit network.

For consumers, dining credits turn restaurant supply into dining capacity that can be understood and combined. For businesses, the same credits can become purchasable, distributable, and trackable organizational benefits. For restaurants, subscribers, business budgets, and venue demand are no longer separate entry points; they become demand sources that can keep converting inside the same network.

Platform value comes from the way these roles strengthen one another: credits create sustained demand, more demand attracts more restaurants, and more restaurants make subscriptions and business purchasing more valuable.

<Section title="From Unclear Value to Broken Fulfillment">
<WideImage src="/images/tabmac/chain.webp" alt="Value chain" width={1920} height={1080} />
When mapping Tabmac's use cases, I treated the problem as one continuous chain: whether users understand the subscription value, whether they can redeem with low friction, whether businesses can purchase and distribute, and whether restaurants can fulfill smoothly.

The first break happens in value comprehension. If users only see discount language, they stay in price comparison instead of understanding how tiers, dining credits, and available restaurants form usable dining capacity. The second break happens during in-store redemption. A restaurant visit cannot carry heavy install, signup, rule-learning, and staff explanation costs. Redemption has to stay light.

The third break happens in organizational purchasing. After a company buys dining benefits, it still needs to manage budget, members, distribution rules, and usage records. The fourth break happens in restaurant fulfillment. Restaurants need stable demand, but joining the platform cannot add heavy staff explanation or backend maintenance work.

<Section title="Core Hypothesis: Use Subscription to Connect Discovery, Redemption, and Fulfillment">
Based on the early problem framing, I summarized Tabmac's product hypothesis:

If Tabmac can use tiers and dining credits to create a clear dining value structure, and keep credits consistent across consumer use, business distribution, and restaurant fulfillment, then subscription becomes more than a purchase entry point. It becomes the system foundation that connects demand, supply, and fulfillment.

This hypothesis became five system principles: make the credit model understandable first, adapt redemption and checkout to real in-store contexts, and preserve management and fulfillment capabilities for businesses and restaurants. Each module needs to strengthen the subscription network rather than only solve its local task.

<Section title="V1: Making the Consumer Loop Clear First">

The first version of Tabmac did not start with backend complexity. It first validated the consumer loop: whether users could understand the subscription and credit model, discover available restaurants, confirm what they could use, and complete redemption or payment before or after the visit.

The main interface needed to turn dining subscription into concrete choices that could be browsed. Users should see restaurants, tiers, available dining credits, and action entry points instead of abstract membership benefits. The map turns credits into real geographic choices, while the store page explains what can be used at that restaurant, what the rules are, and how to redeem.

From an interaction perspective, I kept tiers, remaining credits, and redemption entry points on the same decision path. Users first confirm "what can I use," then move into restaurant details and redemption. On mobile, each page keeps one primary action so filtering, explanation, and payment do not compete on a small screen.

The account page is not secondary in this system. Because Tabmac includes plans, dining credits, business-distributed benefits, campaign rewards, and payment, users need one stable place to review their current plan, remaining credits, history, and what they can do next.

<Section title="Turning Business Purchasing into a Manageable Workflow">
<WideImage src="/images/tabmac/business.webp" alt="Tabmac Business dashboard" width={2048} height={1536} />

Business is not a separate enterprise backend. It extends the same dining credits from individual consumption into organizational purchasing and benefit distribution.

When a company buys dining benefits, the most complex part is not payment. It is what happens after purchase: how benefits are distributed, whether purchases recur, how members use them, and how admins review usage. I separated Bulk Purchase, Recurring Purchase, Distribution, and Teams into clear management tasks instead of compressing every setting into one checkout form.

Admins can complete bulk or recurring purchase first, then decide recipients and team scope. This keeps purchase behavior and organization management separate enough to understand, while making later tracking easier. For restaurants, Business connects the same credit system to organizational budgets, making the Tabmac ecosystem more attractive to join.

The experience is closer to a task-oriented admin tool than a marketing surface. Purchase, distribution, teams, and usage are separated into actions that can be completed independently, with clear status and next-step entry points.

<Section title="Turning Venue Booking from Back-and-forth Communication into Instant Judgment">
<WideImage src="/images/tabmac/venue.webp" alt="Tabmac Venue booking flow" width={2880} height={8667} previewHeight={2048} />

The pain point of traditional venue booking is not that users do not know they want to book. It is that both sides have to keep communicating about capacity, date, minimum spend, menu, service fees, cancellation rules, and special requests. Every condition change can trigger a new quote and confirmation. Users cannot compare venues early, and restaurants spend time on low-certainty inquiries.

Tabmac Venue borrows the structure of hotel booking: choose a venue, time, and party size, review capacity, rules, and pricing, then move toward confirmation. This lets users judge fit earlier instead of submitting a form and waiting for a response.

The key to Venue is not moving a private dining form online. It is turning capacity, time, minimum spend, and rules that used to require multiple rounds of communication into a quote structure that users can evaluate upfront. Instant Quote is the core mechanism: restaurants can use custom rules to influence pricing and availability, while users see actionable pricing before starting the conversation and can compare venue and time options earlier.

Interaction-wise, the Venue flow is split into consecutive steps: choose conditions, review constraints, receive a quote, and continue to confirmation. Users deal with one decision at a time, and restaurants receive requests that are closer to a bookable state.

<Section title="Making Checkout and Redemption Low-friction Entry Points">
In an in-store context, users have little patience. If checkout, redemption, or tipping requires installing the full app, creating an account, or learning complex rules, it directly hurts completion and increases staff explanation cost.

So Tabmac Checkout and Redeem were designed as lightweight entry points. With App Clips, users without the full app can still complete payment. Redeem lets campaign benefits, business benefits, and subscription credits share one redemption path, so benefits from different sources do not become different in-store flows.

Tips were treated as part of payment and fulfillment policy, not an add-on after checkout. Tips need to sit in the same flow as business policy, restaurant settings, and user confirmation. This reduces uncertainty for employees using company benefits and makes service rules clear before final payment.

From an experience design perspective, Checkout and Redeem reduce branching decisions. Users first see the available benefit, then confirm amount, tip, and final state, instead of having the whole subscription model re-explained at the end of payment.

<Section title="From a Feature Set to a Dining Demand System">
<WideImage src="/images/tabmac/connect.webp" alt="Tabmac ecosystem connection diagram" width={2048} height={1152} />

The final Tabmac design is not a feature set. It is a dining demand system that runs around dining credits.

The demand foundation is subscription and dining credits. Users understand dining value through different tiers and combine experiences freely across the partner restaurant network.

Demand expansion comes from Business and Venue. Business purchasing brings dining budgets into organizational contexts, while venue booking brings team events and business demand into the restaurant network.

The fulfillment layer is made of Checkout, Redeem, and Tips. They reduce friction around in-store payment, benefit redemption, and service-rule confirmation, allowing consumers, businesses, and restaurants to complete transactions inside the same system.

When these modules connect, Tabmac's logic becomes clear: dining credits create sustained demand, more demand attracts more restaurants, and more restaurants make subscriptions and business purchasing more valuable.
</Section>

<Section title="A Subscription Platform Has to Fulfill Its Value">
Tabmac changed how I understand subscription product design. A subscription is not just a pricing bundle, and it is not solved by adding more membership benefits to a page. The difficult part is using tiers and quotas to create a value structure that feels clear enough for users, controllable enough for businesses, and valuable enough for restaurants to join.

If viewed only from the consumer side, the problem looks like restaurant discovery and redemption. If viewed only from the business side, it becomes procurement and distribution tooling. If viewed only from the restaurant side, it becomes backend and payment efficiency. Tabmac's key insight is that all of these questions revolve around the same thing: how a subscription network can expand user choice, restaurant supply, and fulfillment capacity at the same time.

In the end, I focused the design on how subscription becomes an ecosystem growth structure, rather than only optimizing individual screens. This project made the product lesson clearer: product design is not only about making entry points understandable. It is about making every module in the network strengthen the value of the others, and making the value promised to users truly fulfillable.

</Section>
